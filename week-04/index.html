Week 4 — The Investor's Desk: Books Become Skills

Mission. Tuesday the desk gets its first client, an investor, and a book's chapter becomes a skill your agent runs: a seeker that finds U.S. spinoffs on EDGAR, prices them with your own code, and ranks a screen by factors you declared, ending with "screens, not recommendations." Thursday the desk works one name from that screen through a worksheet: every fact traced to a filing, a value range against the price, a judgment from a persona you wrote, and a hook that will not let the agent say "done" over a number no filing holds. Your team makes the investor its own, and the live record begins.

Why this matters

Greenblatt's chapter on spinoffs and Klarman's pages on them are not philosophy. They are procedures: who is forced to sell, why the price can fall below value for a few months, where to look, and what to check before you believe it. A skill is where a procedure lives now: a folder your agent reads, with scripts beside the instructions, that runs the same search and the same checks every week and shows every number it used. Your instructor built one this fall for his own accounts. Its parts are the shape you will build: scripts that read EDGAR, a registry of facts established by reading a filing, a report dated by the price date, a worksheet whose every figure cites a filing, evals that run the same question with and without the skill, and the last line of every report, "screens, not recommendations." You will not see that repository. You will build your own from the same public sources, and it will be better where you made it better. One rule comes with the books: a chapter from 1991 is a hypothesis, not a truth. Your ranking is a guess until a check you wrote before seeing any return says otherwise. The live record scores the view on each release; the value range waits years, and eight weeks cannot judge it. Your product manual says so.

You arrive with

An agent team whose process is a graph, with a verifier; your closes reader from this week's list; Checkpoint 2 filed; the two chapters read; one recent Form 10 opened; your factor list written by hand, each factor with its direction and the chapter it came from.

Outcomes

  • Build a seeker skill that finds spinoffs on EDGAR from a start date, keeps a registry of facts with their filings, prices each name with your reader, and ranks a screen by declared factors, never estimating a fact it could not find.
  • Build a worksheet skill that fills a name's facts from its filings, computes owner cash flow and a value range with the arithmetic printed, and lets the persona you wrote judge what the numbers cannot see.
  • Add a hook that refuses "done" when a number in a skill's output is not in a filing, a facts file or a printed computation.
  • Say what the ranking claims, what the value range claims, and which one the hit rate scores.
  • File the team's first live report.

The sessions

  • Session 7 — Lab 07: Your investor. The live record begins; there is no checkout.

Teaching 1 — a chapter is a procedure

Both authors tell the same story. A parent distributes shares of a business to its own holders. Those holders never chose it, and many sell. An index fund must sell it if it is not in the fund's index. Analysts do not cover it yet. So for some months the price can sit below what the business is worth, and nobody has to be wrong for that to happen: the sellers are selling for reasons that have nothing to do with value. Klarman's cases add two more timings: the parent before the distribution, when holders sell it to buy the piece they want; and the leftover after it, when they dump what they did not want. Read as a procedure, the chapter says: find every spinoff; learn who had to sell and whether they have finished; read what the parent left inside it; value it from what it earns, not from what it trades at; and look at the parent too.

The rule is Week 3's again: the search and the checks are deterministic, the judgment is dynamic. Code finds the filings, reads the dates, prices the names and counts the points. A model, from your persona, says why this one may be cheap, or why it is not that story, and what in the next filing would change the view.

Teaching 2 — the filing sequence of a spinoff

A spinoff leaves a trail on EDGAR, and most leave the same one, which is why a script can find them. The parent announces the plan in an 8-K, often months ahead. The new company registers its shares on a Form 10: 10-12B for an exchange listing, 10-12G otherwise. The information statement, the document the parent's holders receive, is attached to it as an exhibit, and it carries the parent, the distribution ratio, the record and distribution dates, the pro forma financials, and what the parent is leaving inside: debt raised whose cash went to the parent, liabilities, the tax agreement that limits buybacks and sales for about two years. Amendments follow. The exchange files a CERT when it approves the listing; when-issued trading starts about a week before the distribution. At the distribution an 8-K is filed, and often by the parent, not by the new company, so a seeker that reads only the new company's list can miss the day it started trading. The new company's own list then begins: Form 4s by insiders, the first 10-Q, the first 10-K, and any of those proves it trades. Other endings exist: a withdrawal, a deregistration because the business was merged away, or an S-1 because the plan became an IPO. Your seeker reads this trail; the facts it establishes go into a registry with the filing and section beside each.

Four facts that bite, each the kind a cold run finds. The first price in a vendor's file is often the when-issued print, days before the distribution; your window starts at the distribution. A 10-12G is filed by many funds and shells that are not spinoffs; a first test is whether the filing holds an information statement, the proof is a parent distributing shares of the business, and the seeker reads anything small before trusting it. The pro forma statements can understate the standalone costs the business will carry, taxes, corporate overhead and interest on the new debt, so the worksheet reads their notes and records a cost it cannot establish as "not known." And index membership on most student data is current only, so the forced-selling story is read from the parent's index and the new company's listing, and said as a reading, not a fact.

Teaching 3 — the screen is a hypothesis

A screen ranks names so you read the likely ones first. The factors come from the chapters: small relative to the parent, so the parent's holders dump it; weak in its first weeks, so the selling is under way; few analysts, so nobody has priced it; insiders buying, which is Form 4 with transaction code P, an open-market or private purchase; small in absolute size. Each one has a direction the chapter predicts. Your factors file states each factor, its weight and its direction, and the report prints every name's points per factor, so a partner can recompute a rank. The weights are yours and they are unvalidated until a check says otherwise, and the report says so.

The check is a procedure too, and it is Thursday's Expand. Write the test before you see a return: which past spinoffs, from which source, entered on which day after the distribution, held how long, compared with what. Then run it once. Count the comparisons you made: twenty cells and five factors are a hundred tests, so about five pass at the five-percent level by chance, and the honest result says which factor passed in the cell you named first. The published evidence, on the readings page, is mixed, and its samples end years ago; whether the effect survived the people who read those papers is the question your check asks of a later sample. A ranking with no check is a story with points.

Teaching 4 — the worksheet

One name, one file, every figure with its filing, its section and its date. The non-negotiables, from the chapters and from the instructor's own worksheet: cite every fact; mark each figure by the statement it came from: historical, pro forma, or reported after the spin, because a Form 10 holds the first two side by side; value as a range, a low and a mid case at least, from owner cash flow, which is after-tax earnings plus depreciation minus capital spending, never a multiple of EBITDA alone; compare the price with the low case; list what the parent pushed into it; look at the parent after the spin; count insiders' open-market purchases; say whether the name can be bought at the purchase size you wrote for your client; and write what is not known. The instructor's worksheet, its headings only, the shape, not your answer:

# [Company] ([TICKER]): spinoff worksheet, [date]
Screens to look at, not a recommendation.
## Facts            parent; distribution date and ratio; price on [date]; market value; index; window ends; rank with its points
## Why it may be cheap   the forced or indifferent seller, or why it is not that story here
## Value range      method and rates stated; owner cash flow; low and mid cases; price against the low case
## What the parent pushed into it   each item with its amount and citation, or "looked in [sections], found none"
## The parent after the spin

## Not known

The persona fills one part: why it may be cheap, and what would change the view. Write it by hand, in the six parts from the Week 3 page, with the chapter beside each criterion and never a copied passage.

Teaching 5 — the hook: code the agent cannot skip

A hook is a command your agent runs at an event: before a tool call, after one, when it is about to say it is done. A skill's instructions can be skipped by a model; a hook cannot. This week's hook reads a skill's output and checks every number: one that claims a tag and a period against the run's facts file, exactly; one that claims a filing and a line against that filing's saved text; one computed from printed inputs by recomputing it; a rate or a weight against the skill's own file. A number of no such kind fails. On a failure the hook refuses and prints the line. At the finishing event a refusal does not end the turn; the agent keeps working, which is the refusal you want. Two things to know before you enable one: a hook runs with your permissions, so read its file first (Codex asks you to trust each hook by name before it runs); and a hook that calls a model is a model call, not a check.

Teaching 6 — two horizons, and what the price already said

The worksheet says two things, and they are not the same claim. The value range is about years; Greenblatt did not promise next week. The view, more or less than the price, is about this release. Only the view enters the hit rate: your code, from the closes your reader pulls, takes the last close before the filing time and the first close after it, and the return between them is the reaction. A view written before the reaction is scored; a view written after it saw the move and is marked "after." Over a season, the share of views that agree with the reaction is a hit rate, and a coin scores about half. And one check before you trust any backtest: ask your model, in a fresh chat with tools off, a figure from a release you have; if it answers right, it may have seen that release, and that quarter is then not a clean test of the model, whatever it says about it. Live releases are the clean test.

Application — the live record's companies

The seeker finds names nobody is covering; the live record reads the names everybody is. Each week your team's desk runs on at least one earnings release filed that week, and the worksheet skill writes its view. The season's story is companies spending on data centers faster than their operations pay for it, and a value worksheet reads that the way it reads a spinoff: cash from operations against capital spending, debt raised and where it went. One trap your code must handle: a 10-Q's cash flow statement is year to date, so a quarter's capital spending is the difference between two filings' lines. A starting set for the partner's picks and the live record, on both sides of the build-out: Oracle, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, CoreWeave, NVIDIA, AMD, Broadcom, Dell, Super Micro, Vertiv. The calendar says which week each reports.

Three levels

  • Must. The seeker with its registry and its ranked screen; the worksheet on one name your partner picked, with the persona you wrote and the hook; a view on the live release; the first live report; and a start on the one thing your team chose to own, at whatever depth Thursday reaches.
  • Expand. A second seeker from a second chapter, stocks below net current asset value, from the facts file your desk already reads; or the factor check, pre-registered, on past spinoffs from WRDS.
  • Ship. Both skills as a plugin your teammates install with one command, with evals that run the same question with and without the skill and grade the answers, and a release tag. A skill someone else can run is the thing this course is for.

Expand and Ship are where your ambition goes, and nobody will tell you which to take.

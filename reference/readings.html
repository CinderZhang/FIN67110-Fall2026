Readings — required and further, by week

Each week's page says which readings are required. This page holds every citation, in one list, and adds further reading. The test for a reading is whether it teaches, not where it lives: good Substack posts sit beside journal articles. Every link was checked on 2026-10-15. Papers behind a paywall open through the Purdue Libraries login.

  • Francis, A., & Zhang, C. X. (2026). Can AI match professional analysts? Evidence from a multi-agent system. Finance Research Letters, 103, 110127. doi.org/10.1016/j.frl.2026.110127, through the Purdue Libraries login.
  • Anthropic (2024). Building effective agents. anthropic.com/research/building-effective-agents.
  • Karpathy, A. (2025). Software Is Changing (Again). Y Combinator AI Startup School. youtube.com/watch?v=LCEmiRjPEtQ.
  • Zhang, C. (2026). The Middle Is for the Machine. The Edges Are for You. cinderzhang.substack.com.
  • Karpathy, A. (2026, October 2). Post on reading what language models write, on X. x.com/karpathy/status/2105819303471976479. The source of "80% of the way to ASD-STE100".
  • ASD (2025). ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English, Issue 9. Request the free standard at asd-ste100.org. The writing rules are what "80% of the way to STE" keeps.
  • danyuchn, asd-ste100-skill: a skill for AI coding agents that applies those rules, with a linter. github.com/danyuchn/asd-ste100-skill. Install it in your agent if you want the rewrite in one request.

  • Further: Osmani, A. (2026, January 28). The 80% Problem in Agentic Coding. addyo.substack.com. Agents write most of the code; the last stretch is the hard part, and "comprehension debt" is what you owe on code you no longer understand. Your Reflect beats are the repayment.

Week 4 — the investor's desk: books become skills, and the AI build-out

The skill and the hook. Required: your own agent's two pages, read on the build day and re-checked before each offering; they say where a skill lives, what its first file must hold, and how a hook stops an action.

  • Claude Code: skills, hooks.
  • Codex: skills, hooks.
  • Antigravity: skills, hooks.
  • Samples, read and not installed: the instructor's FAskills, a plugin whose Python layer scores the company before any persona sees the data; virattt's ai-hedge-fund, investor personas on a graph with a risk manager and a portfolio manager, which pulls from a paid source this course does not use.

The chapters. Required: Greenblatt's Chapter 3 and Klarman's Chapter 10. The procedure the seeker runs is in them.

  • Greenblatt, J. (1997). You Can Be a Stock Market Genius. Simon & Schuster. Chapter 3, "Chips Off the Old Stock: Spinoffs, Partial Spinoffs, and Rights Offerings": who sells a spinoff and why, and the three things to look for (institutions do not want it, insiders do, the transaction reveals something). The Libraries hold it.
  • Klarman, S. A. (1991). Margin of Safety: Risk-Averse Value Investing Strategies for the Thoughtful Investor. HarperCollins. Chapter 10, "Areas of Opportunity for Value Investors: Catalysts, Market Inefficiencies, and Institutional Constraints", the pages on spinoffs: index funds sell what is not in their index; the parent can be the cheap piece. Out of print and never reprinted; ask the Purdue Libraries for an interlibrary loan.
  • Graham, B. (1973). The Intelligent Investor, fourth revised edition, with commentary by J. Zweig (HarperBusiness, 2003). Chapter 15, stock selection for the enterprising investor: stocks below net current asset value, the second seeker; and "special situations" in general terms. Chapter 8, Mr. Market; Chapter 20, margin of safety.

The evidence. Optional. Read the sample period before the result; the question is whether an effect found in one decade survived the people who read about it.

  • Cusatis, P. J., Miles, J. A., & Woolridge, J. R. (1993). Restructuring through spinoffs: the stock market evidence. Journal of Financial Economics, 33(3), 293–311. doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(93)90009-Z. U.S. spinoffs 1965 to 1988; excess returns for subsidiaries and parents over three years.
  • Desai, H., & Jain, P. C. (1999). Firm performance and focus: long-run stock market performance following spinoffs. Journal of Financial Economics, 54(1), 75–101. doi.org/10.1016/S0304-405X(99)00032-X. 1975 to 1991; focus-increasing spinoffs do better.
  • McConnell, J. J., Ozbilgin, M., & Wahal, S. (2001). Spin-offs, ex ante. Journal of Business, 74(2), 245–280. PDF, Purdue. 1989 to 1995; no excess return against matched firms or a three-factor model; "a shaky basis for rejecting" market efficiency. The paper to read against the two above.
  • McConnell, J. J., Sibley, S. E., & Xu, W. (2015). The stock price performance of spin-off subsidiaries, their parents, and the spin-off ETF, 2001–2013. Journal of Portfolio Management, 42(1), 143–152 (abstract). Subsidiaries beat reasonable benchmarks, parents by less; a portfolio of every subsidiary beat the spin-off ETF. A later U.S. sample, read against the 2001 paper.
  • Veld, C., & Veld-Merkoulova, Y. V. (2009). Value creation through spin-offs: a review of the empirical evidence. International Journal of Management Reviews, 11(4), 407–420. doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2370.2008.00243.x. The survey.
  • Further: Boyar Research (2019), Spinoffs have dramatically underperformed: the prior decade's spinoffs trailed the S&P 500. And S&P Dow Jones Indices (2015), the launch of the S&P U.S. Spin-Off Index: companies spun off within four years, a float cap floor, monthly rebalance; the benchmark a factor check can compare against.

EDGAR for spinoffs. The forms the seeker reads, from the SEC's own pages.

  • Form 10: registration of a class of securities under the Exchange Act; 10-12B under Section 12(b), an exchange listing; 10-12G under Section 12(g). The EDGAR form types list names both and CERT, "certification by an exchange approving securities for listing".
  • Staff Legal Bulletin No. 4 (1997): the conditions for a spin-off without Securities Act registration, and the information statement the holders receive. On EDGAR it is the Form 10's exhibit, in practice EX-99.1.
  • Form 8-K: Item 2.01, completion of a disposition of assets; Item 5.01, a change in control; Item 8.01, other events; Item 9.01, exhibits. Which company files which item at a distribution is a thing to read, not assume.
  • Form 4: transaction code P is an open-market or private purchase; grants (A) and tax withholding (F) are not buying.
  • EDGAR full-text search and its FAQ: filings since 2001; quoted phrases, OR, NOT, NEAR. Accessing EDGAR data: ten requests a second, and a User-Agent with a name and an email on every request.

Data for the factor check. The wrds package. CRSP marks spinoffs in its distributions with codes 3762, 3763 and 3764, and names the new security's PERMNO (the CRSP data descriptions guide). S&P 500 membership history on WRDS is CRSP's dsp500list_v2, since Compustat's constituents table was removed in 2020 (a librarian's note).

The value investor, further.

  • Buffett, W. E. (1986). The chairman's letter, appendix, "Purchase-Price Accounting Adjustments and the 'Cash Flow' Fallacy" (berkshirehathaway.com). Owner earnings in three parts: (a) reported earnings, plus (b) depreciation and the other non-cash charges, less (c) the average capital spending the business needs. The worksheet's owner cash flow is the same arithmetic with (c) as reported.
  • Buffett, W. E. (1984). The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville. Hermes, Columbia Business School, Fall 1984. Start here: the essay's record.
  • Fama, E. F., & French, K. R. (2021). The value premium. Review of Asset Pricing Studies, 11(1), 105–121. SSRN. The value premium was much lower in the second half of 1963–2019, and too noisy to say it is gone.
  • Marks, H. (2011). The Most Important Thing. Columbia University Press. Chapter 2, market efficiency and its limitations. His memos are free: oaktreecapital.com/insights/memos.
  • Greenblatt, J. (2010). The Little Book That Still Beats the Market. Wiley. Two ratios, ranked: the baseline a persona must beat.
  • Munger, C. T. (2005). Poor Charlie's Almanack, ed. P. D. Kaufman; Stripe Press edition, 2023.

  • Graham, B., & Dodd, D. L. (2008). Security Analysis, sixth edition. McGraw-Hill. Read Seth Klarman's preface, "The Timeless Wisdom of Graham and Dodd".

  • Koijen, R., & Levy, I. Explaining Markets, the live benchmark. explainingmarkets.ai.

  • Rozenblit, L., & Keil, F. (2002). The misunderstood limits of folk science: an illusion of explanatory depth. Cognitive Science, 26(5), 521–562.
  • Van Nieuwerburgh, S. (2026). Financing the AI Buildout. Brookings Papers on Economic Activity, conference draft, September 2026. PDF.
  • Further: CNBC (2026, July 24). Bond market anxiety is growing over AI capex budgets. cnbc.com.
  • Further: Jain, R. (2026, July 28). The AI Capex Bubble: Bigger Than You Think, Funded by You Don't Know Who. Pinetree Macro, pinetreemacroresearch.substack.com. Private credit, off-balance-sheet debt and circular vendor deals; read it against the Brookings draft and decide which claims a filing can support.
  • Further: Breckinridge Capital Advisors (2026). The price of AI: how capex is rewriting tech balance sheets. breckinridge.com.
  • The filings behind the Oracle application: its 10-Qs, its 10-K and its earnings 8-K, from EDGAR's filing list for Oracle; the page states no figure, the student's skill reads them.

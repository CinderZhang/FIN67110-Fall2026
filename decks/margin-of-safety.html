Investing versus speculation

The investor

Buys a claim on a business and its underlying cash flows. The return comes from what the business earns and what it is worth.

The speculator

Buys a security only because the price will rise. The return comes from what the next buyer pays.

  • The buyer's purpose separates investing from speculation.
  • An asset with no cash flow of its own can only be speculated on.

The margin of safety

  • You buy a security only at a large discount to a conservative value estimate.
  • This discount absorbs the impact when the future turns out worse than you expected.
  • Valuation is an imprecise art, and every analyst makes mistakes.

Illustrative units per share. The numbers are yours: move them. The margin is measured from the low end of the range, never the middle.

Risk is not volatility

  • Risk is the probability of a loss combined with the size of that loss.
  • Your first job is to avoid losing money, not to chase a high return.
  • Volatility only measures how much a market price moves around.
  • A permanent loss happens when the business value was never there.

Three traits of a value investor

Bottom-up

You study one security at a time instead of forecasting the economy.

Absolute performance

You measure against your own standard, not against an index this quarter.

Risk-averse

You ask how much you can lose before you ask what you can make.

Why institutions fail at value

Relative performance

Judged against peers every quarter.

Short horizons

A year is long; a decade is unthinkable.

Fully invested

A rule to buy whatever is popular, with no cash to wait.

Career risk

Standing apart from the crowd and looking wrong alone.

The crowd's constraints are the individual investor's best bargains.

Mr. Market serves you

  • Mr. Market offers to buy or sell securities at a new price every day.
  • You treat his daily price as a service to use, not a verdict to obey.
  • Price is only what the market quotes today. Value is what the business is worth.

Your conservative value is 100 units. You buy at or below 100 minus the margin. You sell when price reaches 100.

Value a business three ways

  • Net present value of conservative future cash flows. Small changes in growth or the discount rate swing the result.
  • Liquidation value: what the assets would fetch if the business sold them.
  • Stock-market value: what comparable public businesses sell for.

Illustrative units. Each method gives a range, never a point. Where the ranges disagree, the most conservative one sets your standard.

Where bargains come from

Forced or uneconomic selling: someone sells for a reason that has nothing to do with value.

Spinoffs

Index funds and institutions sell what they did not choose and what does not fit their mandate.

Bankruptcies and distressed debt

Holders who cannot own defaulted paper sell it, whatever the recovery.

Thrift conversions

Shares sold at a price set by a formula, not by a market.

Small and complex

Size and legal complexity keep most analysts away.

Catalysts close the gap

  • A catalyst is a corporate event that forces the market price toward business value.
  • Liquidations, tender offers, restructurings and completed spinoffs act as catalysts.
  • Partial catalysts unlock part of the value and shorten your waiting time.
ValuePrice

Illustrative. The price path is the same each time; only the catalyst moves.

Cash is a position

Wait

When no security meets your margin-of-safety standard, you hold cash and wait.

Refuse the rule

The crowd stays fully invested at all times. You do not.

Sell at value

You sell when the price reaches your value range. You never fall in love with a holding.

Diversify enough

Enough to survive a mistake, not so much that you own securities you did not study.
